# Finance Advisor Project Grounding Knowledge: Valuation Principles

## Title
Core Equity Valuation Principles & Multiple Analysis Framework

## Purpose
Establishes the analytical framework for evaluating asset valuation multiples and intrinsic value models. It enforces the rule that no valuation metric can be labeled as "cheap" or "expensive" without rigorous context regarding future growth, return on invested capital, sector norms, and capital structure.

## Definitions
- **Trailing P/E**: Market price divided by realized earnings over the past 12 months (LTM).
- **Forward P/E**: Market price divided by consensus projected earnings over the next 12 months (NTM).
- **Price-to-Earnings-to-Growth (PEG)**: The P/E multiple divided by the projected earnings growth rate, providing a shorthand to adjust multiples for speed of expansion.
- **Enterprise Value (EV)**: Total theoretical acquisition cost of a firm ($Market\ Cap + Total\ Debt - Cash$).
- **EV/EBITDA Multiple**: Ratio comparing enterprise value to earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization.
- **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)**: Valuation method forecasting future free cash flows and discounting them back to the present using the weighted average cost of capital (WACC).
- **Relative Valuation**: Valuing an asset by comparing its multiples to historical averages and industry peers.

## Practical Explanation
Valuation is the process of translating operational forecasts and risk assessments into an estimated economic worth. Multiples are shorthand reflections of three underlying variables:
1. Expected future earnings and cash flow growth rate.
2. The predictability and durability of that growth (economic moat).
3. The cost of capital / interest rate regime.

When market participants assign a high P/E to a business, it typically signals expectations of superior future growth, defensive market share, or high return on equity. Conversely, a low multiple may reflect slower growth, cyclical vulnerability, debt distress, or corporate governance risks ("value trap"). Crucially, a high P/E does not automatically mean a stock is overvalued; it must be interpreted relative to growth expectations, industry benchmarks, and profitability dynamics.

## Examples
- **Growth Context**: Company A trades at a 32x forward P/E while growing EPS at 28% annually. Its PEG ratio is approximately 1.14x, suggesting the multiple is largely aligned with its expansion velocity.
- **Value Trap Context**: Company B trades at 7x trailing P/E in a declining legacy sector with shrinking revenues and high debt. The low multiple reflects business risk rather than an automatic discount.
- **Capital Structure Context**: Comparing capital-intensive firms with varying debt loads is cleaner using EV/EBITDA rather than P/E, because EV accounts for net debt obligations.

## Limitations
- Multiples cannot distinguish between sustainable structural advantages and short-term cyclical peaks.
- DCF calculations are hypersensitive to terminal growth and discount rate assumptions.
- Accounting treatments of research & development (expensed vs capitalized) create artificial multiple distortions between tech and industrial firms.

## When This Knowledge Is Useful
- Responding to questions such as "What does P/E mean?", "Is a P/E of 30 too high?", or "How do I value a tech company vs a bank?".
- Grounding discussions of forward multiples, EV/EBITDA comparisons, and valuation relative to growth.
- Structuring valuation sections in decision-support reports.
